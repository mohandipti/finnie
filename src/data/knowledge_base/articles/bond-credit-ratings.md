# Bond Credit Ratings

## What a credit rating is

A **credit rating** is an estimate of the credit risk of a bond, company or government: how likely it is to pay its debts on time. Ratings usually appear as letter grades, such as AAA or BBB. They are issued by third-party **credit rating agencies**, not by the issuer or the SEC.

Agencies registered with the SEC are called **nationally recognized statistical rating organizations (NRSROs)**. The SEC oversees and examines them, but by law it can't regulate the substance of their ratings or their methods.

## The rating scale

Each agency defines its own symbols, but a typical scale runs from **AAA** at the top down to **D**, which indicates default.

- **Investment grade:** at some agencies, BBB- or higher.
- **Non-investment grade:** anything lower. These bonds are also called **high-yield**, **speculative** or "junk" bonds. They generally pay higher interest to compensate investors for the greater risk of default.

**Example:** two companies each issue 10-year bonds. The AA-rated company might pay 5% interest, while a B-rated company might have to pay 8% to attract investors, because there's a greater chance it won't pay back the money.

## What ratings don't tell you

- A rating is **not a guarantee**: even AAA-rated debt sometimes defaults.
- Ratings address **credit risk only**. They don't cover interest rate risk, market risk, liquidity risk or prepayment risk, so a highly rated bond can still lose value when rates rise.
- Ratings can **change at any time**, without warning, at any level. Agencies sometimes issue "outlooks" or "watches" about possible changes, but not before every change. A downgrade usually pushes a bond's price down.
- There's no standard way to measure how accurate ratings are.

## Conflicts of interest

Many rating agencies, including the largest, are **paid by the issuers they rate**, which creates a potential pressure to give higher ratings than warranted. Others are paid by subscribers, usually investors, who may also have interests in particular ratings. NRSROs must disclose and manage these conflicts.

## Using ratings well

Use credit ratings in addition to, not instead of, your own research. For bond funds, the fund's reports show the average credit quality of its holdings.

## Key takeaways

- Ratings estimate the risk that an issuer won't pay; AAA is highest, D means default.
- Bonds below BBB- are high-yield or "junk": higher interest for higher risk.
- Ratings cover credit risk only, can change without warning and aren't guarantees.
