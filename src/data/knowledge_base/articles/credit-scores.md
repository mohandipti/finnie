# Credit Scores: What They Are and What Affects Them

## What a credit score is

A credit score is a prediction of your credit behavior, such as how likely you are to pay a loan back on time, based on the information in your credit reports. Companies calculate it with a mathematical formula called a **scoring model**. Most credit scores range from **300 to 850**.

## How scores are used

Lenders use credit scores to decide whether to offer you a mortgage, credit card, auto loan or other credit, and to set the **interest rate** and **credit limit** you get. Scores are also used for tenant screening and insurance. Usually, a higher score makes it easier to qualify for a loan and may result in a better rate or terms.

**Example:** on a large loan such as a mortgage, a lower interest rate from a higher score can add up to thousands of dollars of savings over the life of the loan.

## What affects your score

Scoring models typically consider:

- your bill-paying history;
- your current unpaid debt;
- the number and types of loan accounts you have;
- how long your accounts have been open;
- how much of your available credit you're using;
- new applications for credit;
- whether you've had a debt sent to collection, a foreclosure or a bankruptcy, and how long ago.

## You have more than one score

There isn't just one credit score. Each score depends on the data used to calculate it and can differ by scoring model (which may depend on the type of loan), by which credit bureau's data is used, and even by the day it was calculated.

## Building a good score

Because scores are built from your credit reports, the habits that help are the ones those reports record: paying every bill on time, keeping balances low compared with your credit limits, and applying for new credit only when you need it. Checking your own credit reports for errors is also important, since a score is only as accurate as the report behind it.

## Key takeaways

- A credit score predicts how likely you are to repay, based on your credit reports.
- Most scores range from 300 to 850; higher scores usually mean better loan terms.
- Payment history, debt levels and how much credit you use are among the main factors.
