# Cost Basis: Figuring Your Gain or Loss

This article describes general US federal rules. IRS Publications 550 and 551 cover special cases.

## What basis is

Your **basis** is generally what you paid for an asset. When you sell, your capital gain or loss is the sale price minus your basis, so knowing your basis is how you avoid paying tax on money that was never profit.

## Basis for stocks and bonds

For stocks and bonds you buy, basis is the **purchase price plus costs** such as commissions and transfer fees.

**Example:** you buy 100 shares at $50 and pay a $5 commission, so your basis is $5,005. You later sell them for $7,000 and pay a $5 commission, receiving $6,995. Your capital gain is $6,995 − $5,005 = $1,990.

## Adjusted basis

Events during ownership can raise or lower your basis, giving an **adjusted basis**:

- **Reinvested dividends** buy new shares, each with its own basis. Forgetting them means paying tax on those dividends twice: once when received and again when you sell.
- **Return of capital** distributions reduce basis.
- A **wash sale** adds the disallowed loss to the basis of the replacement shares.
- For property such as a home, improvements that add value increase basis.

## Assets you didn't buy

- **Inherited** assets generally take the fair market value on the date of the owner's death, often called a "stepped-up" basis.
- **Gifts** generally keep the giver's basis.

**Example:** a parent bought shares for $10,000 that are worth $50,000 at death. An heir's basis is generally $50,000, so selling right away creates little or no gain. If the parent had given the shares as a gift instead, the basis would generally stay $10,000.

## Which shares did you sell?

If you bought the same stock or fund at different times, each purchase (a "lot") has its own basis. Brokers usually sell the oldest shares first unless you choose specific lots. Choosing high-basis lots can reduce a gain; choosing long-held lots can qualify for long-term rates.

## Records and Form 1099-B

Brokers report sales and, for most securities bought in recent years, basis on **Form 1099-B**. Keep your own records of purchases, reinvested dividends and adjustments, especially for older holdings and inherited assets.

## Key takeaways

- Basis is generally what you paid, including commissions; gain or loss is the sale price minus basis.
- Reinvested dividends and other events adjust basis, so track them.
- Inherited assets usually get a basis equal to their value at death; gifts keep the giver's basis.
