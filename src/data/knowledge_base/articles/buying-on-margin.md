# Buying on Margin

## What margin is

Buying **on margin** means borrowing money from a brokerage firm to buy investments, using the investments in the account as collateral. A **margin account** increases buying power, but it also exposes the investor to **larger losses**, and the loan charges interest.

## How margin magnifies results

Suppose a stock bought for $50 rises to $75:

- **Paid in full (cash account):** the $25 gain is a **50%** return on $50.
- **Half borrowed** ($25 cash + $25 margin loan): the same $25 gain is a **100%** return on the $25 invested.

Now suppose the stock falls to $15 instead. In the margin example, the investor loses their entire $25 **and still owes the broker $10**, plus interest. With margin, it's possible to lose more than the amount invested.

## The key rules

- **Minimum margin:** before trading on margin, FINRA requires a deposit of at least **$2,000** or 100% of the purchase price, whichever is less. Some firms require more.
- **Initial margin:** under the Federal Reserve's **Regulation T**, an investor may generally borrow up to **50%** of the purchase price. Some firms require a larger deposit.
- **Maintenance requirement:** after buying, FINRA requires the account to keep equity of at least **25%** of the market value of the margin securities. Many firms set it higher, typically **30% to 40%**.

## Margin calls

If the account's equity falls below the maintenance requirement, the firm can issue a **margin call**, requiring more cash or securities.

Importantly, the brokerage firm can **sell securities in the account without notifying the investor**, potentially at a substantial loss, and the investor generally doesn't get to choose which securities are sold. Firms can also raise their requirements at any time.

## Key takeaways

- Margin amplifies both gains and losses, and losses can exceed the money invested.
- Regulation T allows borrowing up to 50%; FINRA's maintenance minimum is 25%.
- A firm may sell holdings without notice to cover a margin shortfall.
