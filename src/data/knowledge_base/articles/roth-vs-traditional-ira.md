# Roth vs. Traditional IRA

This article describes general US tax rules, which change over time. A tax professional can explain how they apply to a specific situation.

## The core difference: when you pay tax

| | Traditional IRA | Roth IRA |
|---|---|---|
| Contributions | May be tax-deductible now | Made with after-tax money |
| Growth | Tax-deferred | Tax-free |
| Qualified withdrawals | Taxed as ordinary income | Tax-free |
| Required minimum distributions | Yes, starting at a set age | None during the original owner's lifetime |
| Income limits | Deduction may be limited if you or a spouse have a workplace plan | Ability to contribute phases out at higher incomes |

## The tax-rate trade-off

The choice often comes down to comparing your tax rate today with your expected tax rate in retirement:

- If you expect a **higher** rate in retirement, paying tax now (Roth) can be favorable.
- If you expect a **lower** rate in retirement, deferring tax (traditional) can be favorable.

Because future tax rates are uncertain, some people use both types to diversify their tax situation.

## Qualified Roth withdrawals

Roth earnings are tax-free when the withdrawal is "qualified": it must come at least five years after your first Roth IRA contribution, and be made after age 59½, because of disability, or to a beneficiary after death. Roth contributions (not earnings) can generally be withdrawn at any time without tax or penalty, because withdrawals are treated as coming from contributions first.

## Example

Suppose you contribute $5,000:

- **Traditional:** if deductible, your taxable income drops by $5,000 this year. Every dollar withdrawn later is taxed.
- **Roth:** no deduction now. If the $5,000 grows to $40,000, qualified withdrawals of the full $40,000 are tax-free.

## Key takeaways

- Traditional: tax break now, taxed later.
- Roth: taxed now, tax-free later.
- The comparison depends heavily on current and future tax rates, which vary by person.
