# Growth, Value, Income and Blue-Chip Stocks

Investors often group stocks by their characteristics. A stock can fit more than one category, and both common and preferred stocks can fall into them.

## Growth stocks

**Growth stocks** belong to companies whose earnings are growing faster than the market average. They rarely pay dividends, because they reinvest profits in the business; investors buy them hoping the share price will rise (**capital appreciation**). A start-up technology company is a typical example. Growth stocks often have high P/E ratios, reflecting the growth investors expect, and they can fall sharply if that growth disappoints.

## Income stocks

**Income stocks** pay dividends consistently, and investors buy them for the income they generate. An established utility company is a typical example. Their prices often grow more slowly, and dividends are never guaranteed.

## Value stocks

**Value stocks** have a low price-to-earnings ratio, so they are cheaper relative to their earnings than stocks with a higher P/E. They may have fallen out of favor with investors. People buy them hoping the market has overreacted and the price will rebound. The risk is that a low price is sometimes deserved, a situation investors call a "value trap."

## Blue-chip stocks

**Blue-chip stocks** are shares in large, well-known companies with a solid history of growth. They generally pay dividends and are considered more stable than smaller companies, though they can still lose value.

**Example:** a portfolio might combine a fast-growing software company (growth, no dividend), a utility (income), a bank trading at a low P/E after bad news (value) and a large consumer-products company (blue chip). Each behaves differently as markets and the economy change.

## Styles in funds

Many mutual funds and ETFs focus on a style and a company size, such as "large-cap growth" or "small-cap value." Growth and value styles tend to take turns outperforming over different periods, which is one reason investors diversify across both.

## Key takeaways

- Growth stocks reinvest profits and rarely pay dividends; income stocks pay steady dividends.
- Value stocks trade at low prices relative to earnings, sometimes for good reason.
- Mixing styles is one way to diversify, since they perform differently at different times.
