# Retirement Accounts: 401(k) and IRA Basics

This article describes US retirement accounts. Rules and contribution limits change; check IRS.gov for current figures.

## Why retirement accounts exist

Retirement accounts give tax advantages to encourage long-term saving. In exchange, they limit how much you can contribute each year and usually restrict withdrawals before age 59½.

## The 401(k)

A 401(k) is a retirement plan offered by an employer.

- **Payroll contributions:** money is taken directly from your paycheck.
- **Employer match:** many employers add money based on what you contribute, for example 50% of your contributions up to 6% of salary. A match is effectively extra compensation.
- **Vesting:** employer contributions may become fully yours only after a period of employment.
- **Traditional or Roth:** many plans offer pre-tax (traditional) contributions, after-tax (Roth) contributions, or both.

## The IRA

An Individual Retirement Account (IRA) is opened by an individual at a brokerage or bank, not through an employer.

- **Traditional IRA:** contributions may be tax-deductible; withdrawals in retirement are taxed as income.
- **Roth IRA:** contributions are made with after-tax money; qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. Eligibility to contribute depends on income.

IRA contribution limits are much lower than 401(k) limits.

## Early withdrawals

Withdrawals before age 59½ are generally subject to income tax plus a 10% penalty, with some exceptions. This is why retirement accounts are best suited to money meant for the long term.

## Required minimum distributions

Traditional 401(k)s and IRAs require withdrawals to begin at a certain age (73 under current IRS rules), called required minimum distributions (RMDs). Roth IRAs do not require withdrawals during the original owner's lifetime.

## Key takeaways

- 401(k)s are employer plans and may include a match; IRAs are opened individually.
- Traditional accounts give tax benefits now; Roth accounts give tax-free withdrawals later.
- Early withdrawals usually trigger taxes and penalties.
