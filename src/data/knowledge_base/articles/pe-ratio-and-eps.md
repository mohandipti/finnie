# P/E Ratio and Earnings per Share (EPS)

## Earnings per share

**Earnings per share (EPS)** measures a company's profit for each share of common stock. Basic EPS is net income divided by the number of common shares outstanding (any preferred dividends are subtracted from net income first).

**Example:** a company with $100 million of net income and 50 million shares has an EPS of $2.

Because EPS is per share, it lets you compare the profitability of companies of different sizes, and watching EPS over several years shows whether profits are trending up or down.

**Diluted EPS** also counts shares that could be created from stock options, warrants, convertible bonds and convertible preferred stock. It is usually lower than basic EPS and gives a more conservative picture.

## The price-to-earnings ratio

The **P/E ratio** is the stock price divided by EPS. It tells you how much investors are paying for each dollar of a company's earnings.

**Example:** a stock priced at $100 with EPS of $5 has a P/E of 20. Investors are paying $20 for each $1 of annual earnings. If another company's P/E is 25, investors are paying more for each dollar it earns.

## Using P/E sensibly

- **Compare within an industry.** Fast-growing companies tend to have higher P/Es, while mature, slow-growth companies tend to have lower ones, so comparing a tech start-up with a utility tells you little.
- **A low P/E isn't automatically cheap.** "Value" stocks have low P/Es, sometimes because investors have lost interest for a good reason.
- **A high P/E reflects expectations.** Investors may be paying for growth they expect; if it doesn't arrive, the price can fall.
- **Companies without profits** have no meaningful P/E. The **price-to-sales ratio** (market value divided by revenue) is sometimes used instead.

P/E uses either past earnings (trailing P/E) or analysts' estimates of future earnings (forward P/E), so check which one you're looking at.

## Key takeaways

- EPS is a company's profit per share; P/E is the share price divided by EPS.
- P/E shows how much investors pay for a dollar of earnings.
- Compare P/E ratios among similar companies, and use them alongside other measures.
