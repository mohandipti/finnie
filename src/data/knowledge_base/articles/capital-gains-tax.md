# Capital Gains Tax Basics

This article describes general US federal rules for taxable accounts; rates and thresholds change. Retirement accounts such as IRAs and 401(k)s follow different rules.

## What a capital gain is

A capital gain is the profit from selling an investment for more than you paid. If you buy shares for $1,000 and sell them for $1,500, your capital gain is $500. If you sell for less than you paid, you have a capital loss. Gains are generally taxed only when you sell (when they are "realized").

## Short-term vs. long-term

- **Short-term gains:** investments held one year or less. Taxed at ordinary income tax rates.
- **Long-term gains:** investments held more than one year. Taxed at lower federal rates of 0%, 15% or 20%, depending on taxable income.

Holding period can therefore make a meaningful difference to the tax owed on the same profit.

## Using losses

Capital losses can offset capital gains. If losses exceed gains, up to $3,000 of the excess ($1,500 if married filing separately) can currently be deducted against ordinary income each year, with the rest carried forward to future years.

**Example:** a net capital loss of $7,000 on a joint return allows a $3,000 deduction this year; the remaining $4,000 carries forward to the next year.

## Tax-loss harvesting

Tax-loss harvesting means selling an investment at a loss to offset gains elsewhere. The **wash-sale rule** disallows the loss if you buy the same or a substantially identical investment within 30 days before or after the sale, including purchases in an IRA. The disallowed loss isn't lost permanently: it is added to the cost basis of the replacement shares.

## Dividends

Dividends are also taxed. "Qualified" dividends receive the lower long-term rates; other dividends are taxed as ordinary income.

## Key takeaways

- Gains are taxed when you sell, not while you hold.
- Holding more than a year usually qualifies for lower long-term rates.
- Losses can offset gains, subject to the wash-sale rule.
