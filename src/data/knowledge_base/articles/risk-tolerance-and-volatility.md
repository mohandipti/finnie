# Risk Tolerance and Volatility

## What risk means in investing

In investing, risk usually means uncertainty: the chance that results differ from what you expect, including the chance of losing money. Investments with higher expected returns generally carry more risk. This is often called the risk-return trade-off.

## Volatility

Volatility measures how much an investment's price swings up and down. It is commonly expressed as the standard deviation of returns, usually on an annual basis.

For illustration:

- A stock with 30% annual volatility might typically move about 30% above or below its average return in a year.
- A broad bond fund usually has much lower volatility, often in the single digits.

High volatility does not mean an investment is bad, but it means a wider range of possible outcomes, including larger temporary losses.

## Risk tolerance

Risk tolerance is an investor's ability and willingness to lose some or all of an investment in exchange for greater potential returns. It has two parts:

1. **Ability to take risk:** depends on time horizon, income stability, savings and when the money will be needed.
2. **Willingness to take risk:** depends on personality and how a person reacts emotionally to losses.

Common profiles are **conservative** (stability first), **moderate** (balance of growth and stability) and **aggressive** (growth first, accepting larger swings).

## Time horizon and risk

Money needed in one or two years has little time to recover from a downturn, so it is usually kept in stable assets. Money not needed for decades can ride out more ups and downs. Historically, broad stock markets have recovered from declines over long periods, but past performance does not guarantee future results.

## Drawdowns

A drawdown is the decline from a peak to a low point. Losing years are a normal part of stock investing: according to the SEC's Investor.gov, large-company stocks as a group have lost money on average about one out of every three years. In severe downturns, such as 2007–2009, broad US stock markets fell by roughly half from their peak. Understanding this in advance helps investors avoid selling in a panic.

## Key takeaways

- Higher expected return usually comes with higher risk.
- Volatility measures how much prices swing.
- Risk tolerance combines your ability and your willingness to accept losses.
