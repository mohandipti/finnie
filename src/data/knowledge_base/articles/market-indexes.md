# Market Indexes: S&P 500, Dow and Nasdaq

## What an index is

A market index tracks the combined performance of a group of investments, giving a single number that summarizes how part of the market is doing. When news reports say "the market rose 1% today", they usually mean a major index did.

You cannot invest in an index directly, but index funds and ETFs are built to match an index's performance.

## The major US indexes

- **S&P 500:** about 500 large US companies, weighted by market capitalization, so larger companies have more influence. Often used as the benchmark for the overall US stock market.
- **Dow Jones Industrial Average (the Dow):** 30 large, well-known US "blue-chip" companies, chosen by a committee. It is price-weighted, so companies with higher share prices have more influence, regardless of company size.
- **Nasdaq Composite:** nearly all stocks listed on the Nasdaq exchange, several thousand companies. It is heavily weighted toward technology.

## Why weighting matters

In a market-cap-weighted index like the S&P 500, a handful of the largest companies can drive a large share of the index's movement. Two indexes can move differently on the same day because of what they hold and how they weight it.

## Indexes as benchmarks

Investors and fund managers compare their results with a relevant index. A US large-company fund is often compared with the S&P 500; a bond fund is compared with a bond index.

## Key takeaways

- An index summarizes the performance of a group of investments.
- The S&P 500, Dow and Nasdaq differ in what they hold and how they weight it.
- Index funds and ETFs let investors match an index at low cost.
