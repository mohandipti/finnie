# How Dividends Are Taxed

This article describes general US federal rules for dividends received in taxable accounts; dividends inside IRAs and 401(k)s follow the account's rules instead. Details are in IRS Publication 550.

## What a dividend is

A dividend is a distribution a company pays to its shareholders out of its earnings and profits. Mutual funds and ETFs also pass on dividends from the stocks they hold.

## Ordinary vs. qualified dividends

All taxable dividends are **ordinary dividends**. Some of them are also **qualified dividends**, which are taxed at the lower rates that apply to long-term capital gains: 0%, 15% or 20%, depending on taxable income. Ordinary dividends that aren't qualified are taxed at regular income tax rates.

## The holding period

To count as qualified, a dividend must meet several conditions. The main one for investors is the holding period: you must have held the stock for **more than 60 days during the 121-day period that begins 60 days before the ex-dividend date**. The ex-dividend date is the first day on which a new buyer of the stock no longer receives the next dividend.

**Example:** you buy shares a week before the ex-dividend date and sell them a month later. You held them for fewer than 61 days, so the dividend is taxed as ordinary income, even if your tax form lists it as qualified.

## Other kinds of distributions

- **Capital gain distributions:** paid by mutual funds and real estate investment trusts (REITs) when they sell investments at a profit. They are always reported as long-term capital gains.
- **Return of capital:** a return of part of your original investment, not a dividend. It isn't taxed when received but lowers your cost basis; once the basis reaches zero, further amounts are taxed as capital gains.

## Reinvested dividends are still taxed

If dividends are automatically reinvested to buy more shares (a dividend reinvestment plan), you must still report them as income, just as if you'd received cash.

## Form 1099-DIV

Payers generally send Form 1099-DIV for distributions of $10 or more, showing ordinary and qualified dividends separately. Taxable dividends must be reported even without a form.

## Key takeaways

- Qualified dividends get the lower long-term capital gains rates; other dividends are taxed as ordinary income.
- Qualification depends on holding the stock more than 60 days around the ex-dividend date.
- Reinvested dividends are taxable in the year they're paid.
