# Diversification and Asset Allocation

## Don't put all your eggs in one basket

Diversification means spreading money across many investments so that one bad result does not sink your whole portfolio. If you own one company and it fails, you lose everything invested in it. If you own 500 companies and one fails, the impact is small.

## Concentration risk

A portfolio is concentrated when a large share of it sits in one stock, one industry or one type of asset. For example, a portfolio that is 70% in a single stock rises and falls largely with that one company. Diversification reduces this company-specific risk.

## Ways to diversify

- **Across companies:** hold many stocks, often through index funds or ETFs.
- **Across industries:** technology, healthcare, energy, consumer goods and others.
- **Across asset classes:** stocks, bonds and cash behave differently in different conditions.
- **Across countries:** international investments add exposure to other economies.

## Asset allocation

Asset allocation is how a portfolio is divided among asset classes, such as 60% stocks and 40% bonds. It is one of the biggest factors in how much a portfolio's value swings.

- More stocks: higher expected long-term growth, larger ups and downs.
- More bonds and cash: smaller swings, lower expected growth.

The right mix depends on a person's goals, time horizon and comfort with risk.

## Rebalancing

As markets move, an allocation drifts. If stocks rise sharply, a 60/40 portfolio might become 70/30. Rebalancing means periodically selling some of what grew and buying what lagged to return to the target mix.

## What diversification cannot do

Diversification reduces the risk of any single investment hurting you, but it cannot eliminate market-wide risk. In a broad downturn, most stocks fall together.

## Key takeaways

- Diversification spreads risk across many investments.
- Asset allocation sets the balance between growth and stability.
- Diversified portfolios still fluctuate, especially in broad market declines.
