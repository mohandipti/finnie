# Common Investing Biases

## Why behavior matters

Behavioral finance studies how emotions and mental shortcuts lead people to make predictable investing mistakes. Recognizing these biases can be as valuable as understanding markets.

## Loss aversion

People feel the pain of a loss more strongly than the pleasure of an equal gain. This can lead to selling in a panic during downturns, or holding losing investments too long to avoid admitting a loss.

## Herd behavior

Following what everyone else is doing feels safe, but it can mean buying after prices have already soared or selling after they have already crashed.

## Overconfidence

Many investors believe they can pick winning stocks or time the market better than they can. Overconfidence often leads to trading too frequently, which adds costs and taxes.

## Recency bias

Recent events feel more important than they are. After a strong year, people may expect gains to continue; after a crash, they may expect losses to continue, even though short-term market moves are very hard to predict.

## Anchoring

Anchoring means fixating on a specific number, such as the price you paid for a stock, and judging everything relative to it. The market does not know or care what you paid.

## Ways to reduce the impact of biases

- **Have a written plan** with goals and a target asset allocation.
- **Automate contributions**, such as investing a fixed amount every month (dollar-cost averaging).
- **Rebalance on a schedule** rather than in reaction to headlines.
- **Diversify**, so no single decision dominates results.

## Key takeaways

- Emotions and mental shortcuts cause predictable investing mistakes.
- Loss aversion, herding, overconfidence, recency bias and anchoring are common examples.
- Plans, automation and diversification help limit their effect.
