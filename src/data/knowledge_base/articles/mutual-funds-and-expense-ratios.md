# Mutual Funds and Expense Ratios

## What a mutual fund is

A mutual fund pools money from many investors to buy a portfolio of stocks, bonds or other assets. Each investor owns shares of the fund, and the fund's value per share (its net asset value, or NAV) is calculated once a day after the market closes.

## Active vs. index funds

- **Actively managed funds** employ managers who choose investments, trying to beat the market. They usually charge higher fees.
- **Index funds** simply track a market index, such as the S&P 500. Because they follow a passive strategy, they generally have lower fees, lower trading costs and more favorable tax consequences than actively managed funds. An index fund may still trail its index slightly because of fees and tracking error.

## The expense ratio

The expense ratio is the percentage of your investment the fund charges each year to cover its costs. It is deducted automatically from the fund's returns, so you never see a bill.

| Expense ratio | Annual cost per $10,000 |
|---|---|
| 0.05% | $5 |
| 0.50% | $50 |
| 1.00% | $100 |

## Why small fees matter over time

Fees compound just like returns. On $10,000 growing at 7% a year for 30 years, a 0.05% fee leaves about $75,000, while a 1.00% fee leaves about $57,000. The difference comes entirely from fees.

## Other costs to watch

- **Sales loads:** commissions charged when buying (front-end) or selling (back-end) some funds.
- **12b-1 fees:** ongoing marketing and distribution fees included in some expense ratios.
- **Minimum investments:** some funds require an initial amount, such as $1,000 or $3,000.

## Key takeaways

- Mutual funds pool money to buy diversified portfolios.
- Index funds track a market at low cost; active funds try to beat it at higher cost.
- The expense ratio is charged every year, and its effect compounds.
