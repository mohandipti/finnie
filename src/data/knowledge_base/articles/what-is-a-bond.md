# What Is a Bond?

## A loan you make to an issuer

A bond is a loan from an investor to a government or company. In return, the issuer usually pays regular interest (called the coupon) and repays the original amount (the face value or principal) on a set date, called maturity.

**Example:** You buy a 10-year bond with a $1,000 face value and a 4% coupon. You receive $40 a year in interest, and you get $1,000 back after 10 years, provided the issuer does not default.

## Common types of bonds

- **Government bonds:** issued by national governments, such as US Treasury bonds. Generally considered among the safest.
- **Municipal bonds:** issued by states and cities; interest may be exempt from some taxes.
- **Corporate bonds:** issued by companies; usually pay more interest than government bonds to compensate for higher risk.

## Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions

If interest rates rise, newly issued bonds pay more, so existing bonds with lower coupons become less attractive and their prices fall. If rates fall, existing bonds with higher coupons become more valuable. This is called interest rate risk. Bonds with longer maturities, and bonds with lower coupon rates, tend to be more sensitive to rate changes.

Price changes matter mainly if you sell before maturity. If you hold a bond to maturity and the issuer doesn't default, you still receive the stated interest and the face value.

## Risks of bonds

- **Interest rate risk:** prices fall when rates rise.
- **Credit (default) risk:** the issuer may fail to pay. Credit ratings (such as AAA or BB) estimate this risk.
- **Inflation risk:** fixed payments buy less if prices rise faster than the bond's interest.

## Why investors hold bonds

Bonds usually fluctuate less than stocks and provide predictable income. Many portfolios combine stocks and bonds: stocks for long-term growth, bonds for stability.

## Key takeaways

- A bond is a loan that pays interest and returns principal at maturity.
- Bond prices move opposite to interest rates.
- Bonds are generally less volatile than stocks but not risk-free.
