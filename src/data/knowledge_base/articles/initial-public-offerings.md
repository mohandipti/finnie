# Initial Public Offerings (IPOs)

## What an IPO is

An **initial public offering (IPO)** is when a private company first sells its shares to the public. It raises money for the company and creates a trading market for its shares, usually on an exchange such as the New York Stock Exchange or Nasdaq. Investment banks called **underwriters** manage and sell the offering; they gather "indications of interest" from investors and recommend a price, which the company sets.

## The prospectus

The company registers the offering with the SEC, typically on **Form S-1**, which is public on the SEC's EDGAR database. Most of it is the **prospectus**. Because a new public company has no reporting history, the prospectus is often the only source of key information. Sections worth reading:

- **Risk Factors:** what management thinks could significantly hurt the business or the investment.
- **Use of Proceeds:** what the money raised will be used for.
- **Dilution:** the usually large gap between the IPO price and what founders and early investors paid.
- **Financial Statements:** with the independent auditor's opinion.

## How individual investors buy

If you are a client of one of the underwriters, you may be offered shares at the offering price, but underwriters often distribute most shares to institutional investors. Most individual investors instead buy once trading begins, at the market price.

## Risks of new issues

- The **offering price** is a negotiated estimate of the company's value and may bear little relationship to the trading price; it isn't unusual for the price to move sharply on the first day.
- Underwriters may **support the price** in the first days of trading; once that stops, the price may fall well below the offering price.
- **Limited supply:** at first, only IPO shares trade. Shares held by founders, employees and early investors are usually restricted by **lock-up agreements**, often for about 180 days. When they expire, the price may fall if many shares are sold at once.

**Example:** a hot IPO priced at $20 jumps to $35 on its first day because few shares are available. Months later, when lock-ups expire and insiders can sell, it may drift back down.

## Key takeaways

- An IPO is a company's first sale of shares to the public, described in its prospectus.
- Read the risk factors, use of proceeds and dilution sections before investing.
- Early trading can be volatile, and lock-up expirations can push prices down.
