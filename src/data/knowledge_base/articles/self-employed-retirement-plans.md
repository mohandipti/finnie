# Retirement Plans for the Self-Employed: SEP IRAs and Solo 401(k)s

This article describes general US federal rules. Contribution limits change every year, and figuring contributions on self-employment income has special rules; IRS Publication 560 has the details.

People who work for themselves don't have an employer plan, but they can set up their own. Two common choices are a **SEP IRA** and a **one-participant ("solo") 401(k)**.

## SEP IRAs

A **simplified employee pension (SEP)** lets an employer, including a self-employed person, contribute to traditional IRAs (SEP IRAs) for themselves and their employees. A business of any size can set one up.

- Contributions are made by the **employer** only, up to **25% of compensation** (for self-employed people, figured on net earnings from self-employment after certain adjustments), subject to a yearly dollar cap.
- A SEP is simple to set up, using a short form such as Form 5305-SEP, and can be set up as late as the tax filing deadline, including extensions, for that year.
- If you have employees who meet the eligibility rules (generally age 21, worked for you in 3 of the last 5 years and earned a minimum amount), you must contribute for them too, generally at the same rate.

## Solo 401(k)s

A **one-participant 401(k)** is a regular 401(k) covering a business owner with **no employees** (or the owner and spouse). The owner contributes in two roles:

- as **employee**: elective deferrals up to 100% of compensation, up to the annual 401(k) limit (**$24,500** for 2026, plus catch-up contributions from age 50);
- as **employer**: nonelective contributions up to **25% of compensation**.

Combined contributions are subject to an overall yearly limit. A solo 401(k) generally must file **Form 5500-EZ** once its assets reach $250,000 or more at year-end.

## Comparing them

**Example:** a freelancer with modest self-employment income may be able to put away more in a solo 401(k), because the employee deferral (up to $24,500 in 2026) isn't limited to a percentage of income, while SEP contributions are capped at a percentage. A higher earner might reach similar totals with either.

| | SEP IRA | Solo 401(k) |
|---|---|---|
| Who contributes | Employer only | Owner as employee and employer |
| Employees allowed | Yes (must be covered) | No (owner and spouse only) |
| Roth option | Possible (Roth SEP IRA) | Often available |
| Paperwork | Minimal | Form 5500-EZ at $250,000+ |

## Key takeaways

- SEP IRAs allow employer contributions of up to 25% of compensation and are very simple to run.
- Solo 401(k)s let the owner contribute as both employee and employer, often allowing more at lower incomes.
- Check the current year's dollar limits before contributing.
