# How Interest Rate Changes Affect Borrowing and Saving

## Starting point: the Fed's policy rate

When the Federal Reserve raises or lowers its target for the federal funds rate (the rate banks charge each other overnight), other interest rates across the economy tend to follow. How quickly they follow depends on the type of loan or investment.

## Rates that move quickly

- **Short-term rates**, such as yields on Treasury bills, typically move closely with the federal funds rate.
- **Floating-rate (variable) loans**, such as many credit cards, adjustable-rate mortgages and lines of credit, usually adjust soon after a change.
- **Savings yields**, such as rates on savings accounts and money market funds, tend to rise when the Fed raises rates and fall when it cuts them, though often more slowly.

## Rates that depend on expectations

Medium- and longer-term rates, such as **fixed-rate mortgages** and **auto loans**, also respond, but they are shaped by what people *expect* the Fed to do in the future, not only by today's rate.

## Effects on households

- **Lower rates** make borrowing cheaper, so households are more willing to buy homes, cars and other goods, and businesses are more willing to invest.
- **Higher rates** make borrowing more expensive and raise the reward for saving, which tends to slow spending on interest-sensitive purchases like housing and cars.

These effects take time to build up, which is why the Fed watches the economy closely after each change.

## Effects on investments

- **Bonds:** when interest rates rise, prices of existing bonds generally fall, and when rates fall, their prices generally rise.
- **Cash:** higher rates improve returns on savings accounts, CDs and money market funds.
- **Stocks:** higher borrowing costs can weigh on company profits and on how investors value future earnings, but stock prices respond to many other factors too.

## Key takeaways

- Fed rate changes ripple into other interest rates, quickly for variable-rate products and more gradually for fixed-rate loans.
- Lower rates encourage borrowing and spending; higher rates encourage saving.
- Rate changes affect bond prices, cash yields and, indirectly, stocks.
