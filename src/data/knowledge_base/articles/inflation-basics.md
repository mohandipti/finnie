# Inflation Basics and the Fed's 2% Goal

## What inflation is

**Inflation** is the increase in the prices of goods and services over time. It means a broad rise in the overall price level, not just one item getting more expensive. When prices rise, each dollar buys a little less: this is a loss of **purchasing power**.

## A simple example

At 3% inflation per year, something that costs $100 today costs about $103 next year. Over 10 years at 3%, $100 would buy only about what $74 buys today.

This is why money kept in cash can quietly lose value. If a savings account pays 1% while inflation is 3%, the money grows in dollars but loses about 2% of its purchasing power each year.

## How the Fed measures it

The Federal Reserve's preferred measure is the **personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index**, because it covers a wide range of household spending. It also watches other measures, such as the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**.

Policymakers also look at **core** inflation, which excludes food and energy prices. Those prices tend to swing sharply, so leaving them out helps reveal the underlying trend. The Fed still aims to stabilize overall prices, including food and energy.

## Why 2%?

The Fed judges that inflation of **2% per year over the longer run**, measured by the PCE price index, is most consistent with its goals of maximum employment and stable prices. When people can expect inflation to stay low and stable, they can make sound decisions about saving, borrowing and investing.

## What inflation means for investors

- Investment returns are best judged **after inflation** (the "real" return). A 5% return with 3% inflation is about a 2% real gain.
- Inflation that runs higher than expected tends to hurt fixed payments, such as interest on existing bonds.
- Over long periods, investments that grow faster than inflation help preserve and build purchasing power.

## Key takeaways

- Inflation is a broad rise in prices that reduces what money can buy.
- The Fed targets 2% annual inflation, measured by the PCE price index.
- Compare returns after inflation to see real growth.
