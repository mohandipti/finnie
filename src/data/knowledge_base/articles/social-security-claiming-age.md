# Social Security: When to Start Retirement Benefits

Social Security retirement benefits are paid monthly to people who have worked and paid Social Security taxes. One of the biggest decisions is **when to start**, because it permanently affects the size of the monthly check.

## Full retirement age

Your **full retirement age (FRA)** is when you can receive 100% of your benefit. It depends on the year you were born: for anyone **born in 1960 or later, it is 67**. (It was 66 for people born from 1943 to 1954, rising gradually for those born from 1955 to 1959.)

## Starting early

You can start retirement benefits as early as **age 62**, but they are permanently reduced, by **as much as 30%** for someone whose full retirement age is 67. The reduction is calculated for each month you start before your full retirement age.

## Waiting longer

If you wait past full retirement age, **delayed retirement credits** increase your benefit by **8% for each full year** you delay, up to **age 70**. There's no extra increase for waiting beyond 70.

**Example:** someone with a full retirement age of 67 and a benefit of $2,000 a month at 67 would get:

| Start at | Monthly benefit |
|---|---|
| 62 | about $1,400 (30% less) |
| 67 | $2,000 |
| 70 | about $2,480 (24% more) |

## Spouses

Spousal benefits are also reduced if a spouse claims early. They do **not** grow from the worker's delayed retirement credits, although a surviving spouse's benefit can reflect them.

## Things to weigh

There's no single right age. People often consider:

- **health and life expectancy:** waiting pays off more the longer you live;
- **other income and savings:** whether you can afford to wait;
- **whether you're still working:** earnings can temporarily reduce benefits taken before full retirement age;
- **a spouse:** the higher earner's choice affects survivor benefits.

Your personal estimate, based on your earnings record, is available through a my Social Security account at ssa.gov.

## Key takeaways

- Full retirement age is 67 for anyone born in 1960 or later.
- Starting at 62 permanently cuts benefits by up to 30%; each year of delay after full retirement age adds 8%, until 70.
- The best age depends on health, savings, work and family situation.
