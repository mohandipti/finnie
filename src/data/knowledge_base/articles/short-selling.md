# Short Selling

## What a short sale is

A **short sale** is selling a stock you don't own, usually borrowed from your brokerage firm, hoping to buy it back later at a lower price. Short sellers profit if the price falls and lose if it rises. Investors short to profit from an expected decline, or to **hedge** (offset) the risk of a stock they own.

**Example (from Investor.gov):** Company A trades at $60. An investor borrows shares and sells them at $60. The price falls to $40, and the investor buys shares back to return to the lender, making $20 a share, minus costs such as commissions and interest.

## How it works

Your broker lends you the shares from its own inventory, other clients' margin accounts or another lender. As with buying on margin, you:

- pay **interest** on the loan and must follow **margin rules**;
- must pay the lender any **dividends** the stock pays while you're short.

## The risks

- **Losses have no limit.** When you buy a stock, the most you can lose is what you paid. When you short, the price can keep rising, so potential losses are unlimited. If the investor above had to buy back at $90, they'd lose $30 a share.
- **Margin calls:** if the price rises, your broker can demand more cash or securities, or buy back the shares for you at a loss.
- **Short squeezes:** when a heavily shorted stock rises, short sellers rushing to buy it back can push the price even higher.

## Naked short selling and Regulation SHO

In a **"naked" short sale**, the seller doesn't borrow, or arrange to borrow, the shares in time to deliver them within the standard one-business-day settlement period, causing a "failure to deliver." It isn't necessarily illegal, but the SEC's **Regulation SHO**, in effect since 2005, addresses persistent failures to deliver and abusive naked short selling.

## Why short selling exists

Short sellers can add liquidity and help prices reflect bad news, for example by spotting overvalued or fraudulent companies. Their trades are also sometimes the target of manipulation claims, which is why short selling is regulated.

## Key takeaways

- Short selling means selling borrowed shares and buying them back later, hoping for a lower price.
- Losses are unlimited, and you pay interest and any dividends while short.
- It is an advanced strategy that requires a margin account.
