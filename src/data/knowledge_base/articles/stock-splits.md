# Stock Splits and Reverse Stock Splits

## What a stock split is

In a **stock split**, a company increases its number of shares, and the price of each share falls in proportion. Splits can be two-for-one, three-for-two or any other ratio. Companies often split their stock to make shares more affordable to individual investors.

**Example (from the SEC):** you own 100 shares trading at $100 each, worth $10,000. After a two-for-one split, you own 200 shares at $50 each, still worth $10,000.

## What doesn't change

A split doesn't change the company's value or your share of it:

- your total market value stays the same;
- unlike issuing new shares, a split doesn't dilute existing shareholders' ownership;
- dividends per share fall in proportion, so total dividends are unchanged.

A split is like cutting a pizza into more slices: there are more pieces, but the same amount of pizza.

## Reverse stock splits

In a **reverse stock split**, each share is converted into a fraction of a share, reducing the number of shares and raising the price per share.

**Example (from Investor.gov):** in a one-for-ten reverse split, every 10 shares become 1. If you owned 10,000 shares, you'd own 1,000 afterwards, each priced about ten times higher.

Companies do reverse splits to raise a share price they think is too low to attract investors, or to meet an exchange's **minimum bid price** requirement so their shares can stay listed. In some reverse splits, small shareholders are "cashed out", receiving cash instead of fractional shares.

## What to watch for

A reverse split doesn't fix whatever drove the share price down. Investors can lose money from price swings after a reverse split, and a reverse split by a company struggling to stay listed can be a sign of financial trouble. Companies that report to the SEC announce splits in filings such as Forms 8-K, 10-Q and 10-K, available on EDGAR.

## Key takeaways

- A stock split increases the number of shares and lowers the price, without changing the total value.
- A reverse split reduces the number of shares and raises the price.
- Reverse splits are often used to meet exchange listing rules and can signal a struggling company.
