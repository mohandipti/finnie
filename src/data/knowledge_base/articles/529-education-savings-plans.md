# 529 Education Savings Plans

This article describes general US federal rules as published in IRS Publication 970 (2025). Each state runs its own plan, state tax rules differ, and limits change; check the plan and IRS.gov for current details.

## What a 529 plan is

A 529 plan, legally a **qualified tuition program (QTP)**, is a tax-advantaged account for education costs, named after section 529 of the tax code. There are two types:

- **Savings plans:** you contribute to an account that is invested, usually in mutual funds, and its value rises or falls with the markets.
- **Prepaid tuition plans:** you pay for future tuition in advance at an eligible state program or institution.

## The tax benefit

Contributions are **not deductible** on the federal return, though some states offer a deduction. The benefit comes later: investment earnings aren't taxed while in the account, and **no tax is due on withdrawals** used for qualified education expenses.

## Qualified education expenses

- College and other postsecondary costs: tuition, fees, books, supplies and equipment, and room and board for students enrolled at least half-time.
- Elementary and secondary school (K-12) tuition: up to $10,000 a year per beneficiary under the 2025 rules.
- Fees, books, supplies and equipment for registered apprenticeship programs.
- Up to $10,000 (lifetime) of principal or interest on qualified student loans of the beneficiary or a sibling.

## Withdrawals for other purposes

If a withdrawal is more than the beneficiary's qualified education expenses, the **earnings** portion of the excess is taxed as income and generally faces an **additional 10% tax**. The original contributions come back without tax. There are exceptions, for example if the beneficiary receives a tax-free scholarship.

**Example:** a $10,000 non-qualified withdrawal from an account that is 30% earnings: $3,000 is taxable income and the additional tax is $300.

## Flexibility

The beneficiary can be changed to another family member, and money can be rolled over to another 529 plan. Since 2024, limited amounts can also be moved to a Roth IRA for the same beneficiary if the 529 account has been open for more than 15 years and the money was contributed more than 5 years earlier, within the annual Roth IRA contribution limit.

## Key takeaways

- Contributions aren't federally deductible, but growth and qualified withdrawals are tax-free.
- Qualified expenses include college, some K-12 tuition, apprenticeships and limited student loan repayments.
- Earnings withdrawn for other purposes are taxed and usually face an additional 10% tax.
