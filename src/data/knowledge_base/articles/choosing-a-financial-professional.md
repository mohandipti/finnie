# Choosing a Financial Professional

## Types of professionals

You can get help with investing from a **broker** (who buys and sells securities for you, usually paid per transaction), an **investment adviser** (who gives ongoing advice, often for a fee based on the assets managed) or a **financial planner**. Banks, mutual fund companies and insurance companies also offer advice. Services and prices vary, so it pays to compare.

## How they're paid

Professionals aren't working for free. Some fees are obvious, such as a percentage of your account each year; others are less visible, such as commissions or sales charges built into products. Always ask how, and how much, the person is paid, because the way someone is paid can create **conflicts of interest**.

**Example:** a 1% yearly advisory fee on a $100,000 account is $1,000 a year. A broker paid by commission might earn more by recommending one product over another.

## Check registration first

The most important question is whether the person is **registered** with the SEC or a state securities regulator. Brokers, investment advisers and insurance agents must be registered or licensed before they can sell you anything; investing with someone unlicensed is very risky, and many scams involve unregistered people.

- **FINRA BrokerCheck** (brokercheck.finra.org, or 800-289-9999) shows a professional's registration, employment history and disciplinary record.
- The SEC's **Investment Adviser Public Disclosure** database covers investment advisers.
- Your **state securities regulator** can also help.

Scammers sometimes impersonate real registered professionals, so contact the firm through details you find yourself.

## Read the relationship summary

Registered brokers and advisers must give retail investors a **relationship summary (Form CRS)**. It explains the services offered, fees and costs, conflicts of interest, the standard of conduct they must follow, any legal or disciplinary history, and questions to ask. Because every firm uses the same headings, it's easy to compare.

## Look past the titles

Initials after a name don't necessarily mean more qualifications. Some credentials are hard-earned; others are easy to get, and some only allow the person to sell certain products. FINRA lists what each designation requires.

## Key takeaways

- Check registration on BrokerCheck or the SEC's adviser database before working with anyone.
- Ask how the professional is paid, and read the firm's Form CRS.
- Review account statements promptly and object in writing to any transaction you didn't authorize.
