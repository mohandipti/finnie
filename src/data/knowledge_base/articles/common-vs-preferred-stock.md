# Common vs. Preferred Stock

There are two main kinds of stock: common and preferred. Both represent ownership in a company, but they come with different rights.

## Common stock

Common stock is what most people mean by "stock." All publicly traded companies issue it. Common stockholders:

- can **vote** at shareholder meetings, for example to elect the board of directors;
- may receive **dividends**, but the company doesn't have to pay them, and it can cut or stop them at any time;
- benefit most if the company grows, because there's no cap on how much the share price can rise.

## Preferred stock

Preferred stock is a different class of shares with features of both stocks and bonds. Preferred stockholders:

- usually **don't have voting rights**;
- usually receive a **fixed dividend**, similar to a bond's interest payment, which often yields more than the common stock's dividend;
- are paid dividends **before** common stockholders.

Because its dividend is fixed, preferred stock's price tends to behave more like a bond's: it is sensitive to interest rates, and it usually doesn't rise much when the company does well.

## Who gets paid first

If a company goes bankrupt and its assets are sold off, there's a strict order of payment:

1. **bondholders** and other creditors;
2. **preferred stockholders**;
3. **common stockholders**, who get whatever is left, which may be nothing.

**Example:** a company has a common stock paying a $1 dividend that it may raise or cut, and a preferred stock paying a fixed $1.50. If profits fall, the company must pay the preferred dividend before paying anything to common shareholders.

## Share classes

Some companies issue several classes of common stock, such as Class A and Class B, with different voting rights or prices. Check what rights come with the class you buy.

## Key takeaways

- Common stock offers voting rights and unlimited growth potential, but dividends aren't guaranteed.
- Preferred stock usually pays a fixed dividend first but has no vote and limited upside.
- In a bankruptcy, preferred stockholders are paid before common stockholders, but after bondholders.
