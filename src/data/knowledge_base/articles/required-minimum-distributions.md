# Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)

This article describes general US federal rules for account owners; rules for inherited accounts are different. A tax professional can help with specific situations.

## What RMDs are

Money in tax-deferred retirement accounts can't stay there forever. **Required minimum distributions (RMDs)** are the minimum amounts you must withdraw each year, generally starting in the year you reach **age 73**. They apply to traditional IRAs, SEP IRAs, SIMPLE IRAs and employer plans such as 401(k)s and 403(b)s.

**Roth IRAs** and designated Roth accounts in 401(k) and 403(b) plans don't require withdrawals while the owner is alive (beneficiaries who inherit them do face RMD rules).

## Deadlines

- Your **first** RMD is due by **April 1 of the year after** you reach 73.
- Every later RMD is due by **December 31**.

Delaying the first one to April 1 means taking two RMDs in that second year, which can push up your taxable income.

**Still working?** If your employer's plan allows it, you can generally delay RMDs from that plan until April 1 after the year you retire, unless you own 5% or more of the business. This doesn't apply to IRAs.

## How much

Each year's RMD is the account balance on **December 31 of the previous year**, divided by a **distribution period** from the IRS's Uniform Lifetime Table, based on your age. (A different table applies if your sole beneficiary is a spouse more than ten years younger.)

**Example:** an IRA worth $500,000 on December 31, with a distribution period of 26.5, requires a withdrawal of about $18,900 that year ($500,000 ÷ 26.5). You can always withdraw more than the minimum.

## Taxes and penalties

RMDs are taxed as ordinary income, except for any part that was already taxed. If you withdraw less than required, the shortfall may face a **25% excise tax**, reduced to **10%** if corrected within two years. The IRS may waive it for a reasonable error that's being fixed (file Form 5329 with an explanation).

## Key takeaways

- RMDs generally start at age 73 for traditional IRAs and workplace plans.
- The amount is last year-end's balance divided by an IRS life-expectancy factor.
- Missing an RMD can cost 25% of the shortfall; Roth IRAs have no RMDs for the owner.
