# Selling Your Home: The $250,000 / $500,000 Exclusion

This article describes general US federal rules; IRS Publication 523 has full details and worksheets.

## The exclusion

If you sell your **main home** at a profit, you may be able to exclude up to **$250,000** of the gain from income, or up to **$500,000** if you file a joint return with your spouse. Gain above the exclusion is generally taxed as a capital gain.

## Qualifying: the ownership and use tests

You generally need to meet both tests during the **5 years** before the sale:

- **Ownership test:** you owned the home for at least **2 years** (24 months).
- **Use test:** you lived in it as your main home for at least **2 years**.

The two years don't have to be continuous, and the ownership and use periods can be different 2-year stretches. For a joint return, either spouse can meet the ownership test, but **both** must meet the use test to get the full $500,000.

Members of the uniformed services, the Foreign Service and the intelligence community on qualified extended duty can choose to suspend the 5-year period for up to 10 years.

## Figuring the gain

Your gain is the sale price, minus selling costs such as agent commissions, minus your **adjusted basis**: what you paid plus the cost of improvements that add value.

**Example:** a married couple bought their home for $300,000, spent $50,000 on a kitchen and an addition, and sell it for $700,000 after $40,000 of selling costs. Their gain is $700,000 − $40,000 − $350,000 = $310,000. That's under $500,000, so on a joint return none of it is taxable. A single seller with the same numbers could exclude $250,000 and would owe capital gains tax on $60,000.

## Reporting

If you receive **Form 1099-S** for the sale, you must report it even if all the gain is excluded. You must also report it if any gain is taxable, using Schedule D and Form 8949. A loss on the sale of a personal home isn't deductible.

## Key takeaways

- Up to $250,000 of gain ($500,000 for married couples filing jointly) on a main home can be tax-free.
- You generally must have owned and lived in the home for 2 of the last 5 years.
- Keep records of improvements: they raise your basis and lower your gain.
