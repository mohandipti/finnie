# Dollar-Cost Averaging

## What it is

**Dollar-cost averaging** means investing the **same amount of money at regular intervals**, such as $100 every month, regardless of whether prices are up or down. Automatic contributions to a 401(k) from each paycheck are a common example.

## How it works

Because the amount is fixed, you automatically buy **more shares when prices are low** and **fewer shares when prices are high**.

**Example:** investing $100 a month in a fund:

| Month | Price per share | Shares bought |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $10 | 10 |
| 2 | $5 | 20 |
| 3 | $10 | 10 |
| **Total** | | **40 shares for $300** |

The average cost is **$7.50 per share** ($300 ÷ 40), lower than the average price over the three months (about $8.33), because more shares were bought when the price was low.

## Benefits

- Reduces the risk of investing a large lump sum right before a market drop.
- Removes the pressure of trying to **time the market**, which is very hard to do consistently.
- Builds a steady saving habit and can be automated.
- Can be especially helpful in volatile markets.

## Limitations

- It does **not guarantee a profit** or protect against losses in a falling market.
- Markets have historically tended to rise over long periods, so a lump sum invested immediately has often come out ahead. Dollar-cost averaging trades some of that potential for a smoother, less stressful path.
- To work, contributions need to continue through market downturns, which is when they're hardest to keep up.

## Where it fits

The SEC lists dollar-cost averaging among its "ten things to consider" before investing, alongside building an emergency fund, paying off high-interest debt and taking advantage of any employer retirement match.

## Key takeaways

- Invest a fixed amount on a regular schedule, whatever the market is doing.
- It lowers the average cost per share when prices fluctuate and avoids timing decisions.
- It doesn't prevent losses and works best when kept up consistently.
