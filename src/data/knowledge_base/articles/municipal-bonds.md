# Municipal Bonds

## What municipal bonds are

Municipal bonds ("munis") are debt issued by states, cities, counties and other government entities to pay for projects such as schools, highways, hospitals and water systems, or for day-to-day operations. Investors lend money and receive interest, plus their principal back at maturity.

## The tax advantage

Interest on municipal bonds is **often, but not always, exempt from federal income tax**. It may also be exempt from state and local taxes if you live in the state where the bond was issued, or if the bond is issued by a US territory.

Because of this, munis usually pay lower interest rates than taxable bonds. Whether they come out ahead depends on your tax bracket.

**Example:** in a 32% federal tax bracket, a muni paying 3.5% tax-free gives the same after-tax income as a taxable bond paying about 5.1% (3.5% ÷ (1 − 0.32)). In a 12% bracket, the equivalent is only about 4.0%, so a taxable bond might pay more.

Munis offer no extra tax benefit inside an IRA or 401(k), where earnings are already tax-advantaged.

## General obligation vs. revenue bonds

- **General obligation (GO) bonds** aren't secured by specific assets. They're backed by the issuer's **"full faith and credit"** and its power to tax residents to pay bondholders. The details depend on state and local law, and some issuers have limited taxing power.
- **Revenue bonds** are backed by income from a **specific project or source**, such as highway tolls or lease fees, not by taxes. Some are "non-recourse": if the revenue dries up, the issuer isn't required to pay. "Conduit" bonds are issued on behalf of a private borrower, such as a hospital or nonprofit, which is responsible for repayment.

## Risks

- **Credit risk:** municipal defaults are uncommon but do happen, especially for revenue bonds tied to a single project.
- **Interest rate risk:** like other bonds, prices fall when rates rise.
- **Call risk:** many munis can be repaid early by the issuer, usually when rates fall.
- **Liquidity:** many munis trade infrequently, so selling before maturity can be costly.

## Doing research

The Municipal Securities Rulemaking Board's free **EMMA** website (emma.msrb.org) has each bond's **official statement** (its offering document), ongoing disclosures about the issuer's finances, and recent trade prices for checking whether your broker's price is fair.

## Key takeaways

- Municipal bonds fund state and local governments, and their interest is often federally tax-exempt.
- GO bonds are backed by taxing power; revenue bonds by a specific project's income.
- Tax-exempt yields make most sense for investors in higher tax brackets, in taxable accounts.
