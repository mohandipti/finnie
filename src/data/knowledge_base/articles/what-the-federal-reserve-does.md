# What the Federal Reserve Does

## The central bank of the United States

The Federal Reserve (the Fed) is the central bank of the United States. One of its main jobs is setting **monetary policy**: influencing interest rates and financial conditions to keep the economy on track.

## The dual mandate

Congress gives the Fed two main goals, often called the **dual mandate**:

1. **Maximum employment:** the lowest level of unemployment the economy can sustain without pushing inflation up.
2. **Stable prices:** low and steady inflation. The Fed aims for 2% inflation per year over the longer run.

## The federal funds rate

The Fed's main tool is the **federal funds rate**, the interest rate banks charge each other for overnight loans. The Fed doesn't set it directly; instead, the **Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC)**, made up of the Fed's Board of Governors and presidents of the regional Federal Reserve Banks, sets a **target range** for it at regular meetings.

## Easing and tightening

- **Easing:** lowering the target range. Interest rates fall and borrowing gets cheaper. This may be needed when the economy is sluggish or inflation is too low.
- **Tightening:** raising the target range. Interest rates rise and borrowing gets more expensive. This may be needed when the economy is overheating or inflation is too high.

## How it reaches everyday life

A change in the federal funds rate normally moves other interest rates too, such as rates on loans and savings accounts. Those changes affect what households and businesses decide to spend, borrow and invest, which in turn affects jobs and inflation. These effects build up gradually, over months rather than days.

## Why investors watch the Fed

Interest rates influence bond prices, the returns on savings, borrowing costs for companies and how investors value stocks. That's why Fed announcements often move financial markets.

## Key takeaways

- The Fed's goals are maximum employment and stable prices.
- Its main tool is a target range for the federal funds rate, set by the FOMC.
- Rate changes ripple through borrowing, saving and spending over time.
